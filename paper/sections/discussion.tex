\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This work demonstrated that deep learning semantic segmentation models for dense land cover classification at VHSR can be effectively trained with limited labeled data.
Our approach combined a machine-learning driven semantic stratification strategy that uses existing coarse resolution land cover maps to select informative training samples based on their land cover characteristics.
We also leveraged a self-supervised learning pre-training stage that averages large amounts of freely-available unannotated VHSR imagery to enable an image encoder to learn semantically meaningful image representations prior to fine-tuning on the target land cover classification task.
We achieve high land cover classification accuracy with only 1,000 labeled training samples, which is a small fraction of the amount of labeled data typically used to train deep learning models for similar tasks.
For example, the ISPR Potsdam and Vaihingen datasets can be used to create \num{23,750} and \num{8,873} 256 \times 256 pixel training patches, respectively.
Additionally, these datasets only cover small urban areas, and generalization is seldom a primary concern for works that use these datasets.
Our work aims to demonstrate that accurate land cover maps at VHSR can be produced over large areas even under data-scarce scenarios when sufficient care is taken to select training samples that are informative and representative of the target area, and when models are trained using an approach that maximizes the utility of limited labeled data using transfer learning techniques and self-supervised pre-training.
The results of our experiment provide strong evidence to support this claim, and we apply this methodology to produce a \SI{1}{\meter} resolution land cover map for the entire state of Mississippi.

As can be shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inference_results}, the final land cover product produced using our approach provides a comprehensive and detailed characterization of surface phenomena across the entire state of Mississippi, far surpassing the spatial fidelity of existing land cover products such as the NLCD.
Our final land cover map captures fine-scale land cover features such as individual buildings, roads, small bodies of water, precise agricultural field delineations, forest clearings, and mixed urban-vegetation interfaces that are indistinguishable in the NLCD land cover data.
The thematic accuracy assessment results shown in Table~\ref{tab:confusion_matrix} show that our final land cover product achieves exceptional accuracy on the open water and tree canopy/woody vegetation classes in particular, but tends to struggle with the barren land and impervious surfaces classes.
Indeed, there is some substantial confusion between the barren land and impervious surfaces classes, which is likely due to the fact that these surfaces are spectral similar in the NAIP imagery used in this work.
The inclusion of an additional blue band in the model input may help alleviate this issue by providing additional spectral information, but the set of transformations used by BYOL are designed for three-channel inputs and are not directly compatible with four-channel multispectral imagery.
For example, random color jittering is applied, which adjusts the brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue of the input image.
The saturation and hue adjustment transformations are explicitly defined for three-channel imagery as a mapping from the RGB color space to the HSV color space, and thus cannot be directly applied to imagery with more than three bands.
For remote sensing applications, this a significant limitation of BYOL and other transformation-based self-supervised learning methods that rely on image augmentations to learn transformation-invariant image representations, and we suggest that future work should investigate the development of transformation strategies that are directly compatible with multispectral imagery that contain more than three bands.
Reconstruction-based self-supervised learning methods (e.g., masked image modeling) may also be a promising avenue for future research, as these methods do not inherently rely on image augmentations to facilitate feature representation learning.

One notable contributions of this work is the semantic stratification approach to selecting training samples.
The dense prediction nature of semantic segmentation models makes labelling large amounts of training data accurately a time-consuming and labor-intensive task, particulars for VHSR imagery where objects of interest can be small and numerous.
As such, selecting training samples that are representative of the target area is a critical yet oft overlooked aspect of training deep learning models for land cover classification.
To our knowledge, this is the first work to propose a stratification approach explicitly designed to for selecting patch-based training samples for training semantic segmentation models for land cover classification.
We posit that utilizing existing land cover maps (even coarse resolution ones) can serve as a useful guide for selecting strata based on the composition of land cover classes within a population of candidate training patches.
In this way, training samples are selected based on their semantic content to ensure that the training dataset is representative of the land cover characteristics of the target area.
Our results in Section~\ref{sec:results:semantic_stratification} provide strong evidence to support this claim, as models trained using our semantic stratification approach consistently outperformed models trained using random sampling and spatially stratified sampling at larger training dataset sizes.
We note that this approach is not perfect and does not always guarantee that strata are perfectly homogeneous in terms of land cover characteristics, as can be seen in Cluster 122 of Figure~\ref{fig:va_pcs}.
We theorize that the coarse resolution of the NLCD land cover data (\SI{30}{\meter}) relative to the size of our candidate training patches (\SI{256}{\meter}) may contribute to this imprecision, as at these scales, a single patch only contains approximately 73 data points form the NLCD land cover map.
If we opt for a \SI{10}{\meter} resolution land cover map, such as Google's Dynamic World product~\citep{brownDynamicWorldRealtime2022}, ESRI's annual land cover product~\citep{karraGlobalLandUse2021}, or the European Space Agency's WorldCover product~\citep{zanagaESAWorldCover102022}, we would have 655 data points per candidate training patch, potentially providing a more accurate represnetation of land cover composition within each patch.
Additionally, we opted to use only the first two principal components of the NLCD histograms to define our strata, which explained roughly \num{95}\% of the variance in the data, but adding additional principal components could potentially improve the quality of the strata if the remaining variance corresponds to meaningful variation in patch composition.
We hope that this work inspires future research into stratification approaches for selecting training samples for land cover classification, as we believe that the importance of quality training data selection is often overlooked in the context of semantic segmentation models for producing land cover datasets at VHSR.
