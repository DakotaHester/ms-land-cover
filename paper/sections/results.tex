\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Validation of semantic stratification approach for selecting training samples}\label{sec:results:semantic_stratification}

The distribution of the two principal components (PCs) derived from the NLCD histograms for the CBW region in Virginia are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:va_pcs}.
Principal component 1 (PC1) explained \num{66.21}\% of the variance in the data, while principal component 2 (PC2) explained \num{29.11}\% of the variance, for a total of \num{95.32}\% of the variance explained by the first two PCs.
PC1 appeared to primarily distinguish candidate patches based on their urban land cover content: patches with larger positive values of PC1 tend to be placed near urban or built-up regions.
On the other hand, PC2 regions tended to correlate with more vegetated areas.
A qualitative analysis of samples pulled from clusters of PC1 and PC2 are also demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:va_pcs}, demonstrating that samples within the same cluster/stratum tended to have similar land cover characteristics.
For example, samples in cluster 50 had a high degree of urban land cover, while samples in cluster 210 had a mix of forested and agricultural land cover.
This pattern was not necessarily consistent: for example, most samples in cluster 122 contained a mix of water and forested land cover, but one sample contained no water at all.

The mIoU results for models trained using the different sampling strategies and dataset sizes according to the procedure outlined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology:semantic_stratification} are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stratification_results}.
The two-way ANOVA test indicated that both the sampling strategy \(F(2, 81) = 22.26, p < 0.001\) and training dataset size \(F(2, 81) = 116.72, p < 0.001\) have a statistically significant effect on model performance, while the interaction between the two factors is not statistically significant \(F(4, 81) = 2.15, p = 0.082\).
However, the Tukey HSD post-hoc test revealed that the semantic stratification strategy does not yield statistically significant improvements in mIoU at \(N_\text{train} = 250\).
Still, significant improvements in mIoU over both random sampling and spatially stratified sampling were observed at \(N_\text{train} = 500\) and \(N_\text{train} = 750\).
The largest improvements in mIoU were observed at \(N_\text{train} = 750\), where our proposed stratification method yields an average mIoU increase of \num{3.11}\% over random sampling and \num{2.50}\% over spatially stratified sampling.
No significant differences in mIoU were observed between random sampling and spatially stratified sampling at any training dataset size.

\subsection{Evaluation of self-supervised learning approach}\label{sec:results:self_supervised_learning}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:probing_metrics}, using fixed embeddings from a ResNet-101 model pre-trained using BYOL as input to a linear classifier yielded substantial improvements in mIoU over a classifier that uses embeddings from a model pre-trained only on ImageNet.
The largest training dataset size of \(N_\text{train} = 750\) exhibited the largest improvement in mIoU, with the BYOL pre-trained model achieving an mIoU of \num{42.71}\% compared to \num{27.70}\% for the ImageNet pre-trained model, an improvement of \num{15.01}\%.
The embedding model where BYOL pre-training was initialized with ImageNet weights performs marginally worse than the model that begins BYOL pre-training with random weights: the average increase in mIoU over the ImageNet pre-trained model was \num{13.90}\% for the BYOL model with random initialization, compared to \num{13.04}\% for the BYOL model with ImageNet initialization.

\input{figures/probing_metrics.tex}

These trends shifted somewhat when the pre-trained image encoders were used as the backbone in semantic segmentation architectures that were the fine-tuned end-to-end.
Figure~\ref{fig:finetune_metrics} show that while the BYOL models that were first initialized with ImageNet weights prior to BYOL pre-training still outperformed ImageNet models with no additional pre-training, the BYOL models that were first initialized with random weights have more variable performance depending on the specifics of the semantic segmentation architecture.
The BYOL encoder with random initialization only outperformed the ImageNet pre-trained encoder in terms of mean mIoU across all architectures except U-Net and Attention U-Net at \(N_\text{train} = 250\), but only DANet, PAN, and PSPnet at \(N_\text{train} = 500\), and only DANet and PSPNet at \(N_\text{train} = 750\).
However, the BYOL model initialized with ImageNet weights prior to pre-training consitstently outperformed the ImageNet pre-trained model across all architectures and training dataset sizes, echoing the results observed in the linear probing experiment (though to a lesser degree).

\input{figures/finetune_metrics.tex}

The best performing pre-training, semantic segmentation architecture, and training dataset size combination based on mean mIoU across all k-folds was the BYOL model initialized with ImageNet weights used as the backbone in a U-Net architecture trained with \(N_\text{train} = 750\) samples, which achieved a mean mIoU of \num{60.86}\% and a mean overall accuracy of \num{86.43}\%, a \num{1.88}\% and a \num{0.32}\% improvement over the U-Net model using the same training dataset size but with an ImageNet pre-trained backbone, respectively.
The best performing individual model (without averaging across k-folds) was run \#2 of the U-Net model the BYOL backbone with ImageNet initialization fine-tuned using \(N_\text{train} = 500\) samples, achieving an mIoU of \num{63.01}\% and an overall accuracy of \num{87.42}\%.
This was an increase of \num{2.77}\% and {1.32}\% over a similar model with a backbone pre-trained only on ImageNet that was provided the exact same training/validation split.
A qualitative assessment of the inference results from this model is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:visual_assessment}, demonstrating that not only did the model outperform the ImageNet pre-trained model quantitatively, but also produced sharper and more accurate land cover maps that visually appear to be of higher quality and correspond more closely to the true land cover.

\input{figures/visual_assessment.tex}

Despite this specific model's large individual performance, this model was an outlier in terms of performance across all k-folds, as the mean mIoU and overall accuracy across all k-folds for this model was \num{60.36}\% and \num{86.55}\%, respectively, falling short of the best performing configuration in terms of mean mIoU.
As such, the U-Net model with a BYOL backbone initialized with ImageNet weights and fine-tuned using \(N_\text{train} = 750\) samples was considered the best performing model overall and was used to generate the final land cover map for the entire state of Mississippi.
These models were ensembled and used to generate a final land cover map for the entire state of Mississippi using the procedure outlined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology:model_ensembling}.
The confusion matrix for the final thematic accuracy assessment for the statewide land cover map is shown in Table~\ref{tab:confusion_matrix}.
Overall, the final product's mIoU was \num{62.77}\% and overall accuracy was \num{87.14}\%, with the mean class-wise producer's accuracy and user's accuracy being \num{75.60}\% and \num{80.18}\%, respectively. An online dashboard to visualize and interact with the final land cover product can be found at \url{https://www.gcerlab.com/projects/msland}, while the final land cover product can be downloaded from \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16762344}.
The ground truth patches used to train the model can be found at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15670823}.
