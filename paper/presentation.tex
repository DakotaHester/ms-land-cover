\section{Background}

\subsection{A gentle overview of land use and land cover classification}

\begin{frame}{Land use and land cover}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Land use and land cover (LULC) data are among the most common and informative types of geospatial data with respect to environmental monitoring and management.
        \item<2-> \alert{Land cover} refers to the biophysical material covering the Earth's surface (e.g., water, vegetation, soil, structures, etc.).
        \item<3-> \alert{Land use} refers to the human interaction with the land cover (e.g., agriculture, forest practices, urban development, etc.).
        \item<4-> LULC data are used in a wide range of disciplines, including agriculture, forestry, urban planning, and environmental science.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Land use and land cover}
    \begin{figure}
        \centering
        % \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/land_use_land_cover.png}
        \caption{USGS 2023 National Land Cover Database (NLCD) data over Mississippi.}
    \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Classifying LULC}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Prior to the advent of GIS, LULC data was human-interpreted from aerial photography.
        \item<2-> With advent of satellite remote sensing in 1980s, researchers and practitioners began using automated algorithms to classify LULC.
        \item<3-> Most "classical" approaches to LULC classification paradigms were pixel-wise statistical models that used coarse resolution multispectral data.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pixel-wise classification}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Traditional classifiers usually operate by transforming a feature vector into output class probabilities for a given pixel.
        \item<2-> Common data sources include:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item<3-> Surface reflectance data
            \item<4-> Derived spectral indices (i.e., NDVI, EVI, NDWI, etc.)
            \item<5-> Textural information (e.g., gray-level co-occurrence matrices)
            \item<6-> Ancillary information (i.e., topograhy, soil, etc)
        \end{itemize}
        \item<7-> Non-parametric, supervised classifiers are often used (e.g., Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, etc.)
\end{frame}
