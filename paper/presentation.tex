\section{Background}

\subsection{A gentle overview of land use and land cover classification}

\begin{frame}{Land use and land cover}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Land use and land cover (LULC) data are among the most common and informative types of geospatial data with respect to environmental monitoring and management.
        \item<2-> \alert{Land cover} refers to the biophysical material covering the Earth's surface (e.g., water, vegetation, soil, structures, etc.).
        \item<3-> \alert{Land use} refers to the human interaction with the land cover (e.g., agriculture, forest practices, urban development, etc.).
        \item<4-> LULC data are used in a wide range of disciplines, including agriculture, forestry, urban planning, and environmental science.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Land use and land cover}
    \begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/ms_lc_map.pdf}
        \caption{USGS National Land Cover Database (NLCD) data over Mississippi.}
    \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Classifying LULC}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Prior to the advent of GIS, LULC data was human-interpreted from aerial photography.
        \item<2-> With advent of satellite remote sensing in 1980s, researchers and practitioners began using automated algorithms to classify LULC.
        \item<3-> Most "classical" approaches to LULC classification paradigms were pixel-wise statistical models that used coarse resolution multispectral data.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pixel-wise classification}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Traditional classifiers usually operate by transforming a feature vector into output class probabilities for a given pixel.
        \item<2-> Common data sources include:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item<3-> Surface reflectance data
            \item<4-> Derived spectral indices (i.e., NDVI, EVI, NDWI, etc.)
            \item<5-> Textural information (e.g., gray-level co-occurrence matrices)
            \item<6-> Ancillary information (i.e., topograhy, soil, etc)
        \end{itemize}
        \item<7-> Non-parametric, supervised classifiers are often used (e.g., Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, etc.)
\end{frame}

\subsubsection{Semantic Segmentation}

\begin{frame}{Semantic Segmentation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Most CNNs are designed for classification tasks, where the output is simple a single label for the entire image
        \item<2-> An naive approach to land cover classification would be to assign a single label to each tile that is passed through a CNN, where this label represents the dominant land cover class in the tile.
        \item<3-> However, the feature maps learned by the CNN inherently \alert{spatially structured}, and these feature maps can be leveraged to produce dense, pixel-wise classification maps through a process called \alert{semantic segmentation}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Encoder-Decoder Architectures}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> In an encoder-decoder architecture, an \alert{encoder} CNN extracts features from the input such that a \alert{decoder} network can upsample the features to the original input resolution.
        \item<2-> The decoder network learns to generate a pixel-wise classification map from the features extracted by the encoder.
        \item<3-> The most popular encoder-decoder network Architecture is U-Net\cite{wangUNetFormerUNetlikeTransformer2022}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{U-Net}
    \begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/unet_architecture.png}
        \caption{U-Net architecture. Note the "skip" connections between the encoder and the decoder networks. \cite{ronnebergerUNetConvolutionalNetworks2015}.}
    \end{figure}

\begin{frame}{Denoising Autoencoder}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> In addition to semantic segmentation, encoder-decoder networks can be used for denoising images.
        \item<2-> In effect, we can add random noise to an input image, then train the network to predict the original image from the noisy image.
        \item<3-> The network needs to learn \alert{features} that are \alert{robust} to noise while being meaningful for reconstruction.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Transfer Learning}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> While deep learning networks are powerful, their primary drawback is the amount of data required to train them.
        \item<2-> For land cover, this is especcially problematic, as high resolution data is complex and difficult to annotate at scale.
        \item<3-> One approach to mitigate this issue is to use \alert{transfer learning}, where a network can be \alert{pre-trained} on a related task with a large amount of data, then \alert{fine-tuned} on the target task with a smaller amount of data.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Our goal}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Use pre-training strategies in such a way as to reduce the amount of labeled data required for training a deep semantic segmentation model for land cover classification.
        \item<2-> Specifically, we aim to show that land cover classification at very high spatial resolution can be achieved with a small amount of labeled data via the following steps:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item<3-> Pre-train a denoising autoencoder on a large dataset of high resolution imagery from the area of interest.
            \item<4-> Extract the encoder portion of the autoencoder network and use it as the encoder for a U-Net model.
            \item<5-> Perform subsequent pre-training on a smaller dataset of high resolution imagery from a different area with fully annotated land cover data.
            \item<6-> Finally, fine-tune the decoder on a very small dataset of high resolution imagery from the area of interest with fully annotated land cover data.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data Sources}

\begin{frame}{USDA National Agricultural Imagery Program}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> USDA NAIP imagery covers CONUS with 3-year revisit cycle at 0.6 m spatial resolution.
        \item<2-> NAIP imagery is available in 4 spectral bands (RGB + NIR).
        \item<3-> Our study uses 2023 NAIP imagery over Mississippi clipped
        \begin{itemize}
            \item<4-> Color infrared composites (Red, Green, and NIR bands) resampled to 1m spatial resolution
            \item<5-> Imagery is clipped into tiles of 256x256 pixels
            \item<6-> 20\% of images places in pretraining set, and 5\% in pretraining validation set (377,913 and 94,480 tiles, respectively)
            \item<7-> For fine-tuning, 250 tiles total were selected to be labelled (119 in training set, 61 in validation set, and 70 in test set)
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chesapeake Bay Land Cover Data}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> In order to enhance the model's ability to fit the data, we introduce a second pre-training stage using fully annotated data from the Chesapeake Bay Land Cover dataset.
        \item<2-> 2018 NAIP tiles and corresponding land cover data extracted from Fairfax County, VA.
        \item<3-> Why Fairfax County? 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item<4-> High density of hard-to-classify impervious land cover types (i.e., roads, buildings, etc.) 
        \end{itemize}
        \item<5-> Why not directly apply these tiles to the fine-tuning stage?
        \begin{itemize}
            \item<6-> The land cover legend is for Chesapeake Bay --- for examnple, it lacks a Cultivated Crops class.
            \item<7-> High resolution ancillary canopy height data was used to construct the data, which we do not have for Mississippi.
            \item<8-> Generalization of the model to Mississippi is not guaranteed with data from Chesapeake Bay Watershed. 
        \end{itemize}
        \item<5-> 10,220 tiles used for training and 5,110 used for validation.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Pretraining}

\begin{frame}{Our Denoising Autoencoder}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Our encoder network of choice is a ResNet-152 architecture.
        \item<2-> For the Denoising Autoencoder, we extract feature maps produced by each of the four stages of the encoder network, resample these to the oringal input size, concatenate them, and pass them through a single convolutional 1 \(\times\) 1 convolutional layer to produce the input reconstruction.
        \item<3-> While the shallow decoder network inhibits the ability of the network to produce high quality reconstructions, it helps ensure that the encoder learns to extract features.
        \item<4-> To speed up training, our encoder network has been pre-trained on ImageNet, a large corpus of ``natural'' images.
        \item<5-> To evaluate the efficacy of this stage, we compare the performance of our self-supervised model to one that has been only been pre-trained using ImageNet as well as a randomly initialized model.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Supervised Pretraining Stage}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<1-> Once the encoder has been trained, we place it in a U-Net architecture and train the network on the Fairfax County land cover data.
        \item<2-> One important note is that we have the option to \alert{freeze} the encoder during this stage. In doing so, we can reduce the amount of data required to train the full network, but lose the some ability to learn a more task-specific feature representation.
        \item<3-> In our case, we found that there was enough data in the Fairfax County dataset to train the full network without freezing the encoder parameters.
        % \item<3-> In addition to our prior baselines in the pre-training stage, we also compare the performance of our final network against models that have not undergonme this second stage of pre-training, as well as evalutate the effects of freezing the encoder layers.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
